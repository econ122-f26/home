Bending the line

Day 11’s line went below 0. What shape should the probability of default have?

  • Flat near 0 for low balances, rising steeply around $2,000, flattening near 1.
  • Logistic regression fits this S-shaped curve.

The logistic function

Two steps take a customer’s balance \(X\) to a probability:

  1. Score: \(z = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X\). A straight line, so any number. Not a probability.
  2. Probability: \(p(X) = e^{z} / (1 + e^{z})\). The chance of default at balance \(X\), always between 0 and 1.
z = -4 z = -2 z = 0 z = 2 z = 4
p(X) 0.02 0.12 0.50 0.88 0.98
  • \(z = 0\) gives a coin flip. Big \(z\): near 1. Very negative \(z\): near 0.
  • \(\beta_1 > 0\): the curve rises with \(X\). \(\beta_1 < 0\): it falls.

Fitting it in R

glm() (“generalized linear model”) with family = binomial:

fit_bal <- glm(default ~ balance, data = Default, family = binomial)
coef(fit_bal)
##   (Intercept)       balance 
## -10.651330614   0.005498917
  • The criterion is maximum likelihood: choose the coefficients that make the defaults we actually saw as likely as possible. (Least squares plays this role in linear regression.)
  • summary(fit_bal) reads like Day 10’s: estimates, standard errors, and p-values. The z value plays the role of the t value.

1. Reading the coefficients

Making a prediction

What’s the chance a customer with a $1,000 balance defaults? A $2,000 balance?

Plug into \(z\), then into \(p\):

\[z = -10.65 + 0.0055 \times 1000 = -5.15 \qquad p = \frac{e^{-5.15}}{1 + e^{-5.15}} = 0.0058\]

R does both steps with type = "response":

predict(fit_bal, data.frame(balance = c(1000, 2000)), type = "response")
##           1           2 
## 0.005752145 0.585769370

0.6% at $1,000; 59% at $2,000.

What does \(\beta_1\) tell us?

How much does an extra $100 of balance raise the chance of default?

Starting balance Probability After +$100 Rise
$1,000 0.6% 1.0% 0.4 points
$1,500 8.3% 13.6% 5.3 points
$2,000 58.6% 71.0% 12.4 points
  • It depends on where you start. The S-curve is flat at the ends and steep in the middle.
  • So unlike linear regression, \(\beta_1\) is not “the change in \(p\) per unit of \(X\).”
  • What it always tells you: the sign. \(\hat\beta_1 > 0\), so higher balances mean a higher chance of default.

Odds: a constant way to read \(\beta_1\)

The odds compare the chance it happens to the chance it doesn’t: \(\text{odds} = \dfrac{p}{1 - p}\). (A probability of 0.8 is odds of 4 to 1.)

Rearranging the logistic function:

\[\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X\]

  • The log-odds are linear in \(X\). That’s the “linear” part of the model.
  • Raising \(X\) by 1 multiplies the odds by \(e^{\beta_1}\), wherever you start.
  • Balance: an extra $100 multiplies the odds of default by \(e^{100 \times 0.0055} = 1.73\).

2. More than one predictor

Multiple logistic regression

Same idea, more terms in \(z\):

\[z = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \dots + \beta_p X_p \qquad p = \frac{e^{z}}{1 + e^{z}}\]

Term Estimate p-value
Intercept -10.87 < 0.001
balance 0.00574 < 0.001
income 0.000003 0.71
student = Yes -0.65 0.006
  • Each \(\beta_j\) is read holding the other predictors fixed, as on Day 10.
  • Balance matters; income doesn’t add anything once balance is known.

The student puzzle

Are students more or less likely to default?

Model Student coefficient Reading
default ~ student +0.40 Students default more: 4.3% vs 2.9%
default ~ balance + income + student -0.65 At the same balance and income, students default less: 5.8% vs 10.5%, with a $1,500 balance and 40k income

Both are right. They answer different questions.

Why the sign flips

  • Students carry higher balances (average $988 vs $772), and high balances drive default.
  • Alone, student absorbs the balance effect: Day 10’s omitted variable bias.
  • Which one to use? A lender who knows nothing else: a student is riskier. A lender comparing two customers with the same balance: the student is safer.

3. From probabilities to classes

Classify with a cutoff

Does logistic regression beat Day 11’s balance rule?

Fit default ~ balance + student. The bank denies a new loan (predicts Yes) when \(p > 0.5\).

Classifier Denied Error rate Recall Precision F1
Always No 0 3.3% 0% — 0.00
Day 11 rule: balance > $2,000 106 2.8% 24% 76% 0.37
Logistic, p > 0.5 144 2.7% 32% 73% 0.44
  • Logistic catches more defaulters (32% vs 24%) at similar precision.
  • But it still misses 68% of defaulters.
  • The model fits the probabilities. The 0.5 cutoff is our choice, and we can move it.

Choosing the cutoff

Two ways to get a loan wrong. Which way should the cutoff move?

  • False negative: lend to someone who defaults. Lose the loan. Worse.
  • False positive: turn away someone who’d repay. Lose some interest.
Classifier Denied Error rate Recall Precision F1
Logistic, p > 0.5 144 2.7% 32% 73% 0.44
Logistic, p > 0.2 479 4.1% 61% 42% 0.50
  • Down. Fewer false negatives, more false positives.
  • The error rate rises, but it treats both mistakes as equally bad.
  • Easy to explain: “deny anyone with at least a 20% chance of default.”

Summary

Logistic regression models the probability of a class: \(p = \dfrac{e^{z}}{1 + e^{z}}\) with \(z = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \dots + \beta_p X_p\).

  • \(p\) always stays between 0 and 1. Fit with glm(..., family = binomial).
  • Predict with predict(..., type = "response"), or plug in by hand.
  • The sign of \(\beta_j\) gives the direction. The change in \(p\) depends on where you start; the odds change by a constant factor \(e^{\beta_j}\).
  • With several predictors, read each holding the others fixed. Leaving one out can flip a sign (the student puzzle).
  • Turn \(p\) into Yes/No with a cutoff, chosen by what each mistake costs.
