1. What is statistical learning?

The setup

We observe an outcome \(Y\) and some inputs \(X = (X_1, \dots, X_p)\), and assume

\[Y = f(X) + \varepsilon\]

Piece Name(s) In the Auto example
Y response, outcome, dependent variable mpg
X predictors, features, inputs horsepower
f the systematic part — unknown how mileage really depends on power
ε error: everything else, mean zero driver habits, tires, measurement

Statistical learning = the set of methods for estimating \(f\). We call the estimate \(\hat f\), and its predictions \(\hat Y = \hat f(X)\).

What \(f\) and \(\varepsilon\) look like

How does a car’s mileage depend on its horsepower? That dependence is \(f\).

Blue: our estimate of \(f\). Red: one car’s \(\varepsilon\), a Datsun 510 that gets 11.6 mpg more than the line predicts.

Why estimate \(f\)? Two different goals

Prediction: you need a good \(\hat Y\); \(\hat f\) can be a black box.

  • Will this borrower default? Which customers will cancel next month?

Inference: you need to know how \(Y\) depends on each \(X\).

  • How much does an extra year of schooling raise wages?
  • Does horsepower cost imports more mileage? (Day 8’s interaction)

Most economics is inference; most industry data science is prediction. The goal decides the method.

Flexibility vs. interpretability

Flexibility = how many different shapes a method can take.

Method Flexibility Interpretability We cover it
Linear / logistic regression low high Days 10-12
Decision trees medium medium Days 15-16
KNN, random forests, boosting high low Days 13, 17-18
  • For inference, you usually want the interpretable end: a coefficient you can explain.
  • For prediction, it’s tempting to reach for the most flexible method.
  • Today’s punchline: more flexible is not automatically more accurate, even when prediction is all you care about.

The vocabulary of the rest of the course

Supervised learning: we observe \(Y\) for each case, and learn to predict it.

  • Regression: \(Y\) is a number (wage, mpg, price).
  • Classification: \(Y\) is a category (default / no default, spam / not spam).

Unsupervised learning: no \(Y\) at all. Look for structure in \(X\) alone.

  • Clustering: group similar customers, products, counties (Days 19-20).
  • Dimension reduction: summarize 50 variables in 2 or 3 (PCA, Day 21).

Quick check: predicting whether a firm goes bankrupt next year — which kind? Grouping 300 stocks by how their returns move together — which kind?

2. How do we know a model is good?

Scoring a fit: mean squared error

To say whether a model is good, we need a score for how far off it is.

  • For each observation, the miss is \(y_i - \hat f(x_i)\): that point’s \(\varepsilon_i\), as best our model can tell.
  • Square the misses and average them:

\[\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - \hat f(x_i)\big)^2\]

  • Lower is better. Squaring stops + and − misses from cancelling.

So if we shrink the misses, do we shrink \(\varepsilon\)?

Can we make \(\varepsilon\) smaller?

Fit a line and a very flexible curve to 20 cars. Red: each car’s miss.

The misses all but vanish: MSE 18.2 for the line, 0.04 for the curve. Did we just eliminate \(\varepsilon\)?

Check it on cars it hasn’t seen

Same two fits. Orange: the 328 other cars, which neither fit was allowed to see.

On the new cars, the flexible curve is worse: MSE 36.6 vs. 23.9 for the line. The spike near 130 hp fits one car’s \(\varepsilon\). A new car there has a different \(\varepsilon\).

What just happened

No, we didn’t eliminate \(\varepsilon\). The curve memorized the \(\varepsilon\) of these 20 cars, and new cars have their own.

Each miss has two parts:

  • Reducible: \(\hat f\) isn’t the true \(f\). A better model can shrink this.
  • Irreducible: \(\varepsilon\). No model can shrink this.

So score a model on data it hasn’t seen:

  • Training MSE: on the data used to fit \(\hat f\). Rewards memorizing.
  • Test MSE: on new data. This is the one that matters.

A dial for flexibility: polynomial degree

To study this, we need a way to turn flexibility up one notch at a time.

\[\text{mpg} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{hp} + \beta_2\,\text{hp}^2 + \dots + \beta_d\,\text{hp}^d\]

  • Degree \(d\) = the highest power of horsepower in the model.
  • \(d = 1\) is a straight line; each extra power adds a bend.
  • In R: lm(mpg ~ poly(horsepower, d), data = ...)

Training vs. test MSE, degrees 1 to 10

Randomly split the 392 cars in half. Fit each degree on one half; score it on the other.

  • Training MSE falls at every step: 22.0 → 15.2.
  • Test MSE bottoms out at degree 2 (20.2), then climbs to 23.3 at degree 10.

Overfitting

Overfitting: more flexibility lowers training MSE but raises test MSE.

  • Past degree 2, the extra wiggles chase the \(\varepsilon\) of these 196 cars.
  • A model that only looks better on training data hasn’t proven anything. Judge it on test data.

But degree 1 was bad too. There are two ways to miss:

  • Too rigid (underfitting): misses the same way whichever cars you draw.
  • Too jumpy (overfitting): changes with which cars you happened to draw.

Test MSE tells us which model wins. Bias-variance tells us why, and what to change when no model is good enough.

3. The bias-variance trade-off

A thought experiment

Say we predict a 5C student’s GPA from hours studied.

  • Population: all 5C students. The true \(f\) lives here.
  • One training set: this semester’s ECON 122 class, which gives one \(\hat f\).
  • Next semester’s class is a different draw, so it gives a different \(\hat f\).

Now imagine fitting the same method every semester, for many semesters:

  • Bias: how far the average \(\hat f\) is from the true \(f\).
  • Variance: how much the \(\hat f\)’s disagree from semester to semester.

Both describe the method across many draws, not one fitted model.

Seeing it: a world where we know \(f\)

Simulated truth (dashed); fits from 30 training sets of 20 points each:

  • Degree 1: all agree, all miss. High bias, low variance.
  • Degree 3: agree and track the truth. Low bias, low variance.
  • Degree 12: right on average, each chases noise. Low bias, high variance.

Test error = bias² + variance + irreducible

At a new point \(x_0\), averaging over training sets:

\[E\big[(y_0 - \hat f(x_0))^2\big] = \text{Bias}\big[\hat f(x_0)\big]^2 + \text{Var}\big[\hat f(x_0)\big] + \text{Var}(\varepsilon)\]

Bias² collapses by degree 3; after that, flexibility buys only variance. Best: degree 3.

Summary

Statistical learning: estimate \(f\) in \(Y = f(X) + \varepsilon\), for prediction or inference.

Is a model good?

  • Judge it by test MSE. Training MSE always falls as flexibility grows.
  • Overfitting: better training fit, worse test fit.
  • No model can remove \(\varepsilon\).

Bias-variance: Test MSE = Bias² + Variance + Var(\(\varepsilon\))

  • Bias: the average fit misses \(f\). Variance: the fit changes across training sets.
  • The best flexibility is where the sum is smallest.
