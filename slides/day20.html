Where we left off

  • K-means (Day 19): pick \(K\), then find the \(K\) clusters with the smallest within-cluster variation.
  • Two problems:
    • We have to choose \(K\) before we see any clusters.
    • Changing \(K\) can reshuffle everything. On Day 19’s colleges, going from K = 3 to K = 4, none of the 3 clusters simply split in two: colleges moved between all of them.
  • Hierarchical clustering: build one tree that holds the clusters for every \(K\) at once. Choose \(K\) afterwards, by looking at the tree.

Nine points

  • Start with every point as its own cluster: 9 clusters.
  • Which two points would you join first?

The algorithm (ISL Algorithm 12.3)

  1. Start with \(n\) clusters, one point each. Compute the distance between every pair.
  2. Fuse the two closest clusters. Record the distance at which they fused.
  3. Compute the distance from the new cluster to every other cluster.
  4. Repeat 2–3 until everything is one cluster.
  • Bottom-up: \(n\) clusters → \(n - 1\) → … → 1. That’s \(n - 1\) fusions.
  • Step 3 needs a rule for the distance between groups of points.

The first fusions

  • 5 and 7 (distance 0.36), then 1 and 6 (0.71), then 8 joins {5, 7} (1.30), then 4 joins {1, 6} (1.49).
  • How far is 8 from the group {5, 7}? It’s 1.00 from 5 and 1.30 from 7.

Linkage: distance between groups

Linkage Distance between groups A and B 8 to {5, 7}
Complete (R’s default) the largest distance from a point in A to a point in B 1.30
Single the smallest such distance 1.00
Average the average of all the distances 1.15
Centroid the distance between the two centroids (as in k-means)
  • Complete linkage: 8 is 1.30 from {5, 7}, its distance to the farther point, 7.
  • ISL: complete and average are the usual choices; single tends to make long chains (we’ll see one soon).

The whole tree: a dendrogram

  • Each leaf at the bottom is a point. Two branches join at the height where they fused.
  • Bottom: 5 and 7 at 0.36. Top: the last two clusters at 5.37.

Reading the tree

  • Is 9 more similar to 2 or to 5?
  • Is 9 more similar to 2 or to 1?

Reading the tree: height, not left-to-right

  • 9 joins {2, 5, 7, 8} all at once, at height 3.33. The tree can’t tell whether 9 is closer to 2 or to 5.
    • Next to each other at the bottom means nothing: each fusion can be drawn either way round. (Actually: 9 to 2 is 2.84, 9 to 5 is 3.20.)
  • 9 joins 1 only at the top (5.37), so 9 is more similar to 2 than to 1.
  • Rule: two points are as similar as the height where their branches first meet.

Cutting the tree

  • A horizontal cut at height 2.8 crosses 3 branches: 3 clusters. Lower cut, more clusters.
  • cutree(hc, k = 3) picks the height for you; cutree(hc, h = 2.8) cuts at that height.

One tree, every K

  • Cut a little lower and one cluster splits in two; nothing else moves. The clusters are nested.
  • Day 19’s 777 colleges, standardized: where each K = 3 cluster’s colleges go at K = 4.

Linkage matters: the world’s cities

The same 4,251 cities as Day 19, longitude and latitude only. Cut at K = 6.

  • Complete: six regions, a lot like Day 19’s k-means.
  • Single: 3,331 cities in the Old World, 906 in the Americas, 11 in New Zealand, and Anchorage, Honolulu, Perth each alone. Why?

Why single linkage chains

  • Single linkage fuses two groups when any two of their points are close.
  • Cities are dense from Lisbon to Tokyo: a chain of near neighbors joins them all, long before the groups look like regions.
  • So the last clusters to join are the isolated cities, far from every other city.
  • Useful for finding outliers; usually not what we want for groups. Complete and average linkage give more balanced clusters.

Choices that matter

  • Distance: Euclidean, as in KNN and k-means. Standardize first, unless the units should count (Day 19).
    • ISL 12.4.3 also uses correlation-based distance: two shoppers are close if they buy the same kinds of things, even if one buys more of everything.
  • Linkage: complete (default), average, or single. Different trees.
  • Where to cut: look for a height with a big gap between fusions, and think about the use.
  • No CV error to settle any of these. Try a few and see if the answer holds up.

Hierarchical clustering in R

Z <- scale(X)                          # standardize the numeric columns
d <- dist(Z)                           # distances between every pair of rows
hc <- hclust(d, method = "complete")   # or "average", "single"
plot(hc, labels = names, cex = 0.6)    # the dendrogram
clusters <- cutree(hc, k = 3)          # cluster number for each row, K = 3
clusters <- cutree(hc, h = 5)          # or: cut at height 5
  • dist() on \(n\) rows computes \(n(n-1)/2\) distances: 777 colleges is 301,476, fine; 1,000,000 rows would be about \(5 \times 10^{11}\), too many.
  • cutree() returns cluster numbers, like km$cluster: add them with mutate().

Summary

  • Hierarchical clustering: start with every point alone, fuse the two closest clusters, repeat.
  • Linkage sets the distance between groups: complete (largest), average, single (smallest; chains).
  • Dendrogram: similarity = the height where two branches first meet, not left-to-right order.
  • Cut the tree to get clusters; one tree gives every K, nested.
  • Standardize first; no CV error, so check that the clusters hold up.
