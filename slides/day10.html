1. Fitting a line

The model

Simple linear regression assumes \(f\) is a straight line:

\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \varepsilon\]

Piece Name In the Auto example
β0 intercept: predicted Y when X = 0 mpg of a 0-hp car
β1 slope: change in Y when X rises by 1 mpg lost per extra horsepower
ε error, as on Day 9 everything else about the car
  • \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) are the coefficients. They are unknown.
  • We estimate them from data and write the estimates with hats: \(\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1\).

Which line?

Many lines pass through this cloud. Which one is “best”?

Score each line with Day 9’s MSE, and pick the lowest.

Residuals: the misses get a name

For each car \(i\), a line gives a fitted value \(\hat y_i = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_i\).

The residual \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\) is what Day 9 called the “miss.”

Least squares

Least squares picks the \(\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1\) with the lowest training MSE:

\[\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i\big)^2\]

  • ISL drops the \(\frac{1}{n}\) and calls the sum the residual sum of squares: RSS = n × MSE. The same line minimizes both.
  • Calculus gives a formula, so no searching is needed:

\[\hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2}, \qquad \hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x\]

Fitting the line in R

lm() (“linear model”) finds the least squares line for us.

fit_hp <- lm(mpg ~ horsepower, data = Auto)
coef(fit_hp)
## (Intercept)  horsepower 
##  39.9358610  -0.1578447

Reading the coefficients

  • Slope: each extra horsepower is associated with 0.158 fewer mpg, or about 1.6 mpg per 10 hp.
  • Intercept: a 0-hp car would get 39.9 mpg.
    • Extrapolated: the least powerful car in the data has 46 hp.
    • Still needed to pin down where the line sits.

Standard errors and confidence intervals

In practice we get one sample, but we can still estimate that spread.

  • Standard error \(\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\): how much \(\hat\beta_1\) would typically move from sample to sample.
    • Smaller with more data, more spread in \(X\), and less noise.
  • 95% confidence interval: about \(\hat\beta_1 \pm 2\cdot\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\).
    • Built this way, the interval captures the true \(\beta_1\) in 95% of samples.
confint(fit_hp)
##                 2.5 %     97.5 %
## (Intercept) 38.525212 41.3465103
## horsepower  -0.170517 -0.1451725

The true mpg cost of one horsepower is plausibly between 0.145 and 0.171.

Is there a relationship at all?

A hypothesis test asks whether the slope could really be zero.

  • \(H_0\): \(\beta_1 = 0\), so \(X\) has no linear relationship with \(Y\).
  • t-statistic \(= \hat\beta_1 / \text{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\): how many SEs the estimate sits from 0.
  • p-value: if \(H_0\) were true, the probability of a \(|t|\) at least this large.
    • Small p-value (say < 0.05): reject \(H_0\); the relationship is unlikely to be chance.
  • For horsepower: \(t =\) -24.5. The p-value is essentially 0.

The 95% CI and the test agree: the CI excludes 0 exactly when \(p < 0.05\).

Fit measure 1: residual standard error

How far off is a typical prediction?

Residual standard error (RSE): the typical distance between a car and the line, in the units of \(Y\).

  • RSE = 4.9: a typical car’s mpg is about 5 mpg above or below the line.
  • Is that big? Compare it to \(Y\) itself: the average car gets 23.4 mpg, so a typical miss is about 21% of that.
  • Because it’s in the units of \(Y\), whether an RSE is “good” depends on the problem: 5 mpg is large; $5 on a house price is tiny.

Fit measure 2: \(R^2\)

How much of the variation in mpg does horsepower explain?

\(R^2\) = the share of the left panel’s squared misses that the line removes.

  • \(R^2 =\) 0.61: horsepower explains 61% of the variation in mpg.
  • 0 = no better than guessing the average; 1 = every car exactly on the line.
  • No units, so unlike RSE it can be compared across different outcomes.

3. More than one predictor

Is it the horsepower, or the weight?

Powerful cars tend to be heavy. Is horsepower costing mileage, or weight?

Correlation 0.86. The simple regression can’t tell their effects apart.

Multiple linear regression

Give each predictor its own slope:

\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \dots + \beta_p X_p + \varepsilon\]

  • \(\beta_j\) = change in \(Y\) when \(X_j\) rises by 1, holding the other predictors fixed.
  • Still fit by least squares: choose all the \(\hat\beta\)’s to minimize RSS.
  • In R, add predictors with +:
fit_both <- lm(mpg ~ horsepower + weight, data = Auto)
coef(fit_both)
##  (Intercept)   horsepower       weight 
## 45.640210840 -0.047302863 -0.005794157

The horsepower slope shrinks

Model Horsepower slope Its SE R²
mpg ~ horsepower -0.158 0.0064 0.61
mpg ~ horsepower + weight -0.047 0.0111 0.71
  • Alone, horsepower gets credit for weight’s effect too.
  • Comparing cars of the same weight, an extra 10 hp costs only about 0.5 mpg, not 1.6.
  • Omitted variable bias: leave out a predictor that is correlated with \(X\) and affects \(Y\), and \(X\)’s slope absorbs its effect.

Predicting, with honest uncertainty

What mpg should we expect from a 98-hp car? (98 hp is about average.)

new_car <- data.frame(horsepower = 98)
predict(fit_hp, new_car, interval = "confidence")
##        fit      lwr      upr
## 1 24.46708 23.97308 24.96108
predict(fit_hp, new_car, interval = "prediction")
##        fit     lwr      upr
## 1 24.46708 14.8094 34.12476
  • Confidence interval: the average mpg of all 98-hp cars. Reflects only uncertainty about the line.
  • Prediction interval: one 98-hp car, which can land well off the line. A typical miss is RSE ≈ 4.9 mpg, so this interval is much wider.
  • Same center for both. More data narrows the CI, but the PI never gets much narrower than ±2·RSE: Day 9’s irreducible error.

4. Checking the fit

Is a straight line right?

Do the residuals show a pattern the line missed?

A residual plot puts fitted values on the x-axis and residuals on the y-axis.

The U-shape says the truth bends. Adding horsepower² (Day 9’s degree 2) removes most of it. Still “linear” regression: linear in the \(\beta\)’s.

Summary

Linear regression: \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \dots + \beta_p X_p + \varepsilon\), fit by least squares (lowest training MSE).

  • Interpret \(\hat\beta_j\): change in \(Y\) per unit of \(X_j\), holding the other predictors fixed.
  • Uncertainty: SEs, CIs, and p-values ask whether a slope could be 0.
  • Fit: RSE (typical miss, in units of \(Y\)) and \(R^2\) (share of variation explained).
  • Omitted variables can bias a slope; adding the right one changes the story.
  • Check the residual plot before trusting the line.
